A quick note: I'm not a finance professional. I'm sharing what I've learned, in plain language, so it's easier for the next person. Nothing here is financial advice.

Interest rates show up in almost every money decision: loans, savings, bonds, stock values. If you understand what a rate does, a lot of financial news starts to make sense. This post covers what a rate is, who moves it, and what happens when it falls.

## What an interest rate is

An interest rate is the price of using someone else's money. If you borrow, you pay it. If you lend or save, you earn it.

Banks sit in the middle. People deposit savings, and the bank lends that money to borrowers at a higher rate than it pays savers. When rates move, both sides of that arrangement move with them.

## Who moves rates

In the US, the Federal Reserve sets a target for the federal funds rate, the rate banks charge each other for overnight loans. That one rate influences many others: credit cards, car loans, mortgages and savings accounts. The Fed's job is to keep prices stable and employment high, and moving that target is one of its main tools.

## What lower rates tend to do

When the Fed lowers its target, three things tend to follow.

![Diagram: the Fed lowers its target rate, and three paths lead to stock values that can rise](/what-lower-rates-do.svg)

1. **Borrowing gets cheaper.** Loans cost less, so households and businesses borrow and spend more. More spending supports growth, and growth supports company earnings.
2. **Savings and bonds pay less.** When safer options pay less, stocks look more attractive by comparison, so some investors move toward them.
3. **The discount rate falls.** In [The Price of Time](/posts/the-price-of-time), money later is worth less than money now, and the discount rate sets how much less. A lower rate shrinks that gap.

Here is a made-up example of the third one. A payment of $100 that arrives in one year is worth $95.24 today at a 5% rate. At a 10% rate, it is worth $90.91. Same payment, same date, but a lower rate makes it worth more today. Stock valuations work the same way, because they add up future cash flows.

Higher rates push the whole chain the other way: borrowing gets more expensive, savings pay more, and future cash flows are worth less today.

## What can go wrong

- **Stocks are not guaranteed to rise.** The Fed often cuts rates because the economy is weak. In that case, stocks can fall anyway.
- **Effects take time.** A rate change can take months to show up in spending and hiring.
- **Other forces matter.** Inflation, company earnings and global events all move prices too.
