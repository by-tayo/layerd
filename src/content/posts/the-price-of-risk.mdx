In [the intro](/posts/risk-and-time) we landed on one idea: most financial decisions involve risk, and their costs and benefits arrive at different times. That splits into two toolkits. This post builds the first one — return, risk, correlation, and how portfolios actually behave.

The second toolkit, time, gets its own post. This one is about the uncertainty.

## Return: what you actually earned

Over one period, an asset pays you in two ways. The price moves, and it might hand you income along the way — a dividend, a coupon, rent. Add both and divide by what you put in:

```
R = (P_end − P_start + C) / P_start
```

where `C` is the income received during the period.

**Worked example.** You buy a share at $100, it's worth $108 a year later, and it paid a $3 dividend.

```
Capital gain yield  =  ($108 − $100) / $100  =  8%
Dividend yield      =  $3 / $100             =  3%
Total return        =  11%
```

Splitting it that way matters more than it looks. Two stocks can both return 11%, but one did it entirely through price and the other leaned on the dividend — different risk, different tax treatment, different thing to rely on. And to get the dollar figure, you just scale: an 11% return on $8,000 invested is $880 of profit.

## Expected return: what you might earn

Returns you've already earned are facts. Returns you're deciding about are not — they're a distribution. If you can describe the possible outcomes and how likely each is, the **expected return** is the probability-weighted average:

```
E(R) = Σ  pₛ × Rₛ
```

**Worked example.** A stock you think has a 25% chance of a strong year (+28%), a 50% chance of an average one (+10%), and a 25% chance of a bad one (−12%):

```
E(R) = 0.25(28%) + 0.50(10%) + 0.25(−12%) = 9%
```

That 9% is not a number you'll ever actually receive. You'll get 28, or 10, or −12. The expected return is the center of gravity of the outcomes, not a prediction of any one of them.

## Historical return: the estimate you usually have instead

In practice you rarely have clean probabilities. What you have is a track record, and you use its average as your best guess at the expected return:

```
R̄ = (R₁ + R₂ + … + R_T) / T
```

This is a simple arithmetic average of the period returns. It's a serviceable estimate of what to expect next period — with one caveat worth carrying into the time post: the arithmetic average is *not* what your money actually compounded to over the whole stretch. A year of +50% followed by a year of −50% averages to zero and leaves you down 25%. Averages describe a single period; compounding describes a sequence.

## Risk: how wrong the estimate can be

Expected return tells you the center. Risk is the spread around it — and the standard measure is **variance**, the probability-weighted average of squared distances from the mean:

```
σ²  =  Σ  pₛ (Rₛ − r̂)²        (from a distribution)

σ²  =  (1 / (T − 1))  Σ (Rₜ − R̄)²   (from historical data)
```

Squaring does two jobs: it makes deviations in both directions count as risk, and it punishes big misses far more than small ones. Then take the square root to get back to normal units — the **standard deviation**, also called volatility:

```
σ = √σ²
```

**Worked example.** For the stock above, with E(R) = 9%:

```
σ² = 0.25(28 − 9)² + 0.50(10 − 9)² + 0.25(−12 − 9)²
   = 0.25(361) + 0.50(1) + 0.25(441)
   = 201  (%²)

σ  = √201 = 14.18%
```

So: expect 9%, give or take about 14 points in a typical year. That "give or take" is the entire cost of holding the thing.

Standard deviation is the default risk measure because it's simple and additive in the way you'll need next. What it misses is worth knowing: it treats a 20% surprise gain as exactly as "risky" as a 20% loss, and it understates how often extreme moves actually happen. It's a good first number, not the last word.

## Portfolios: return is a weighted average

Almost nobody holds one asset. Once you hold several, the weight of each one is its share of the total:

```
wᵢ = (market value of asset i) / (market value of the whole portfolio)
```

And the portfolio's return — or expected return — is just the weighted average of the pieces:

```
R_p = Σ wᵢ Rᵢ            E(R_p) = Σ wᵢ E(Rᵢ)
```

Nothing surprising here. Put 70% in something expected to return 9% and 30% in something expected to return 4%, and the portfolio's expected return is `0.7(9%) + 0.3(4%) = 7.5%`. Return combines linearly.

## Portfolios: risk is not a weighted average

Risk is where it gets interesting, and it's the one genuinely free lunch in finance.

The naive guess would be that a 70/30 mix of a 18%-volatility asset and a 7%-volatility asset has volatility `0.7(18%) + 0.3(7%) = 14.7%`. It doesn't. The actual number depends on how the two assets move *relative to each other*, measured by **correlation**:

```
Corr(A, B)  =  Cov(A, B) / (σ_A σ_B)
```

Correlation runs from +1 (they move in perfect lockstep) through 0 (no relationship) to −1 (one zigs exactly when the other zags). For a two-asset portfolio:

```
σ_p²  =  w_A² σ_A²  +  w_B² σ_B²  +  2 w_A w_B · Corr(A,B) · σ_A σ_B
```

The first two terms are each asset's own risk, scaled by weight. The third term — the cross term — is the whole story. It's the only place correlation enters, and it's what makes the portfolio's risk drift below the weighted average.

**Worked example.** The 70/30 mix above (σ of 18% and 7%), at a correlation of 0.15:

```
σ_p² = 0.7²(18²) + 0.3²(7²) + 2(0.7)(0.3)(0.15)(18)(7)
     = 158.76 + 4.41 + 7.94
     = 171.11

σ_p  = √171.11 = 13.08%
```

Same 7.5% expected return as before. But the risk came in at 13.08% instead of the 14.7% you'd get by averaging — a reduction you didn't pay for. It came entirely from the two assets not moving together.

Push the correlation around and hold everything else fixed:

```
Corr = +1.0   →   σ_p = 14.70%   (no benefit — this is the weighted average)
Corr = +0.5   →   σ_p = 13.77%
Corr = +0.15  →   σ_p = 13.08%
Corr =  0.0   →   σ_p = 12.77%
Corr = −0.5   →   σ_p = 11.69%
Corr = −1.0   →   σ_p = 10.50%
```

The lower the correlation, the more the combined risk falls below what the parts would suggest. At +1 there's no diversification at all; the portfolio is just a blend. Everywhere below +1, you're getting risk reduction for free.

For three assets the formula grows a cross term for every pair:

```
σ_p² = Σ wᵢ² σᵢ²  +  Σ  2 wᵢ wⱼ · Corr(i,j) · σᵢ σⱼ   (over each pair i, j)
```

Same mechanism, more bookkeeping. As you add holdings, the pairwise cross terms come to dominate the sum — which is why, past a certain point, portfolio risk is almost entirely about how your holdings relate to each other, not how volatile any one of them is.

## The risk that actually matters

Here's the payoff, and it reframes the question you should be asking.

A holding's standalone volatility is not the risk it brings to a portfolio you already own. What it brings is the part of its movement that lines up with everything else you hold. That's its **relevant risk**:

```
Relevant risk of asset i  =  σᵢ × Corr(i, portfolio)
```

And total portfolio risk is just the weighted sum of those contributions:

```
σ_p  =  Σ  wᵢ · σᵢ · Corr(i, portfolio)
```

Run the numbers on a single position with 30% volatility:

```
Corr with your portfolio = 0.8   →   relevant risk = 24%
Corr with your portfolio = 0.1   →   relevant risk = 3%
```

Same 30%-volatility asset. In one portfolio it's a major risk contributor; in another it's almost noise — because in the second one, its ups and downs mostly cancel against the rest of what you hold. A wildly volatile asset that doesn't track your portfolio can *lower* your total risk.

So "is this stock risky?" has no answer on its own. The question is always "risky relative to what I already hold?" And that's the hinge into the rest of finance: markets don't pay you for risk you could have diversified away by holding it alongside other things. They pay you for the relevant part — the risk that's still there after diversification. That compensation is the **required return**, and it's the number the second half of the primer runs on.

## What you can actually do with this

Four moves:

1. **Measure what you earned** — price change plus income, over what you put in. Split it into capital gain and income yield; they're not the same kind of money.
2. **Turn a view of the future into two numbers** — probability-weight the outcomes for the expected return `E(R)`, and take the spread around it for the risk `σ`.
3. **Combine holdings** — portfolio expected return is the weighted average of the pieces; portfolio risk is *less* than that weighted average whenever the pieces move less than perfectly together.
4. **Judge a new position by what it adds** — its relevant risk, `σᵢ × Corr(i, portfolio)`, not its standalone volatility.

The output of all of this is a required return: the compensation the relevant risk demands. Feed that into the other toolkit and it becomes the discount rate — the price you put on waiting.

```
risk  →  required return  →  discount rate  →  present value  →  decision
```

Get the risk assessment wrong and everything downstream is wrong with it. The math is exact; the risk judgment it runs on is not.
